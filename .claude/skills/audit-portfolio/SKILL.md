# Case Study Audit

Audit portfolio case studies the way a hiring manager scans them, then report concrete, prioritized fixes. The core insight driving every check: **case studies are scanned, not read** — recruiters spend under 10 seconds on the first pass and 30-60 seconds on a deep look. Every finding should be framed against that reality.

The case studies live in the project knowledge — use what is in context. Audit each case study found there separately, then add a portfolio-level summary if there is more than one. Only note limitations (e.g. images not viewable) when they genuinely affect a score.

## Step 1 — Score against the framework

Score each dimension 1-5. Justify every score with a specific quote or observation from the case study — never a generic comment. A score without evidence is worthless to the reader.

### 1. The 30-second scan test (weight: high)
Simulate a scan: read only the title, headings, first line of each section, and any visual descriptions, for the equivalent of 30 seconds. Then answer: what is the project, what did this person do, what was the result? If any of the three is unclear, this dimension fails regardless of how good the full text is.

### 2. Outcome-first structure
The final product and result should appear at the top, not after the process. Red flags: long intro before any work is shown, "the goal was to improve the user experience"-style generic openings, process presented chronologically with the outcome buried at the end.

### 3. Specific problem framing
The opening must name a concrete business risk or user pain point — who was hurting, what was at stake, why it mattered. Generic mission statements score 1-2.

### 4. Stated role and team context
The author's individual contribution must be explicit: their role, the team structure, key stakeholders, and how decisions involving others were made. Hiring managers (Uber's interview practice is the reference here) probe exactly this; a case study that says "we" throughout without isolating the author's part scores low.

### 5. Decision storytelling, not artifact dumping
Look for 1-3 explicit decision points: alternatives considered, the tradeoff, why the chosen path won. Both failure modes score low: a wall of every wireframe ever made ("art exhibition"), and a grid of final screens with no reasoning. Compressed process with visible thinking scores high.

### 6. Evidence of impact
Best: metrics tied to the project's KPIs. Acceptable: honest qualitative outcomes — user feedback, stakeholder adoption, fewer support tickets, what changed after launch. Score 1 if there is no outcome at all; do not penalize missing hard numbers when credible qualitative evidence is present.

### 7. Text-to-visual balance and length
The best case studies have surprisingly little text. Estimate the word count; flag walls of text, missing captions, and giant undifferentiated screen grids. Reward motion, short clips, before/after comparisons, and captioned images that carry the story.

### 8. Template sameness and voice
Flag the bootcamp template ("Challenge → User Journey → Personas → Sketches → Result") applied mechanically, and stock phrases like "crafting solutions through strategic storytelling", "empathize-define-ideate", or persona boards that visibly informed nothing. Reward a distinct voice and magazine-feature framing.

### 9. Honesty and reflection
A short takeaways section with a real lesson or admitted limitation signals seniority. Absence is a minor deduction; fake humility ("I learned to always put the user first") is a bigger one.

### 10. Positioning fit (portfolio level, when auditing multiple)
Do the case studies collectively tell one coherent story about what kind of designer this is (B2B, systems-heavy, startup generalist...)? Does the hero statement match what the work actually shows? The work always wins over the tagline — say so when they conflict.

## Step 2 — Write the audit report

Use this structure per case study:

```
# Audit: [Case study title]

## Verdict
One paragraph: overall score (X/50), the single biggest problem, and what already works.

## 30-second scan result
What a recruiter would (and would not) retain after 30 seconds.

## Scorecard
Dimension-by-dimension score with one-line evidence each.

## Top 3 fixes
Ranked by impact-to-effort. Each fix: what to change, why (tie back to the
scan-not-read reality), and a concrete rewrite example using the case study's
own content — e.g. rewrite their actual opening line, don't describe how to.

## Optional polish
Smaller items, briefly.
```

For multi-case-study audits, end with a **Portfolio summary**: positioning coherence, which case study to lead with, which one to cut or shorten, and repeated weaknesses across studies.

Rules for the report:
- Be direct but kind — this is someone's work and job search. Lead the verdict with what works before the biggest problem.
- Every criticism gets a fix. Never leave a "this is weak" without a "do this instead".
- Quote the case study's actual sentences when critiquing writing; propose replacement sentences in the author's voice.
- Calibrate to seniority if known: juniors are excused thinner metrics but not unclear roles; seniors are held to impact and decision-quality standards.
- Do not invent facts for rewrites. Rewrite examples may restructure and sharpen only what the case study already claims; mark any suggested addition that needs real data as `[add your real number/outcome here]`.
- If the case study is genuinely strong, say so and keep the report short. Do not manufacture problems to fill the template.

## Reference benchmarks (for calibrating scores)

- Recruiter first-pass: <10 seconds; deep look: 30-60 seconds average time-on-page.
- Portfolio size norms: 2-3 case studies (junior), 4-5 (mid/senior), max ~5 projects total.
- Core ingredients recruiters expect: problem definition, KPIs, team structure, process, metrics, takeaways.
- 2025-2026 format trends: outcome-led "showcases" over long process docs; 30-90s video of the solution in action; before/after sliders; screen recordings of old vs new.
